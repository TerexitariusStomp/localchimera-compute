How a plan is found

A plan is a point: one number per activity. The limits carve out a convex region and harmony is concave on it, so there is exactly one best answer — or one flat face of equally-best answers — and every path upward reaches it. That is what makes the whole question tractable, and it is why the word search misleads here: nothing is explored, sampled, or enumerated, and no space of candidate plans is ever sifted.

What the engine does is solve. The plan sits at one point, and every tension reads its own slack off that same point — that is what “position along these curves” means. A limit reads how much room is left; a desire reads how much of the want is met. From those readings the engine assembles two things: the total pull Σ φ′·a, and the total curvature Σ φ″·aaᵀ. Then it solves for the step at which those pulls would cancel — H⁻¹·pull — and moves there. On a quadratic that lands on the answer in a single step; on this objective it takes a handful. The curves are the local model from which the next position is computed.

There is a trajectory, and it is the last panel’s central path. The barrier weight starts high, which parks the plan at the analytic centre — as far from every limit as it can get, barely listening to the objective at all. Then the weight is swept down and the plan slides toward the optimum. That is a homotopy: a sequence of easy problems deforming into the hard one, each solved from where the last finished.

One genuine search does exist, and only one: the indivisible choice — a mill that must run at scale or not at all. That really is combinatorial, and it is handled by branch and bound, where every node is one of these solves and the certificate is what licenses discarding whole branches unexplored.

Every panel here is wired to that one coordinate — slack — and any of them will drive the others. Sweep the 1-D program and its level becomes three slacks, which light up on the curves above. Sweep the polytope and its point becomes five, one per limit. Sweep a curve and that slack is shown wherever it falls: as levels in the program, and in the polytope as a line running parallel to each limit, exactly that much room in from it. They are the same quantity read four ways, with no translation between pictures.

So which picture is right? Solving simultaneous equations by successive approximation — which is literally what it is, the equations being the KKT conditions. Or, if you want something physical: a ball settling in a bowl whose walls grow infinitely steep as you near them, except this ball reads the curvature and jumps straight to where it would have come to rest. A maze, a search tree and hill-climbing with restarts all picture somewhere to get stuck; convexity means there is nowhere.

Two activities, and the shape the limits actually make

the feasible set is a polytope; the barrier keeps the plan inside it; the path is how the solver crosses — and here the optimum is an edge, not a corner

Every limit is a straight cut; together they make a polytope. The barrier is what keeps the plan strictly inside it, and the path is where the plan sits as the barrier weight is swept down — from the analytic centre, where every limit is equally far away, toward the optimum. The objective here runs parallel to an edge, so there is no single best corner: the whole edge is optimal, and the path arrives at the middle of it. That is a face, and it is the ordinary case rather than a curiosity.

Marked at the point they are built, so the rule holds by construction — the solver sees everything, the certificate sees only the world. A method tension may steer the climb; it may never appear in what a plan claims to have proven. The distinction earns its keep twice over: certifying against the coordination pull would credit a scope for satisfying scaffolding the coordinator erected, and reading a reconciliation cap as a real limit sends a bound to infinity.
